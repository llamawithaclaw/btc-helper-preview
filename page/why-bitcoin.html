Why Bitcoin?

Some people see Bitcoin as money whose eventual supply is capped and whose issuance follows a published schedule rather than anyone's decision. Here is what that means, what it doesn't mean, and why it may not be for you.

This page comes after sound money and household resilience. Bitcoin is optional; it is not the definition of sound money.

The problem it tries to address

Claim In most modern economies, most money is created by central banks and commercial banks, and its supply can be expanded within policy and regulatory limits. Bitcoin was designed so that issuance follows rules in its software that no single party can change on its own.

Its properties

  • Claim Scheduled, capped issuance: under the current consensus rules, new bitcoin are issued on a schedule (the issuance per block halves every 210,000 blocks, about every four years), and total supply approaches a cap of about 21 million. The rules are enforced by the people who run the software; changing them would need wide adoption, and incompatible changes can split the network.
  • Claim Bearer-style control: whoever controls the private keys can spend the coins. You can hold the keys yourself (self-custody) or use a custodian; then your rights and your exposure to that company depend on the arrangement and on the law where you live.
  • Claim Portable and divisible: it can be sent across borders where access is available, and one bitcoin divides into 100 million units (satoshis).
  • Claim Verifiable: anyone running a fully validating node (which needs a computer, storage and an internet connection) can check the supply rules and every transaction.

Claim Several of these properties line up with sound-money ideals. That is why Bitcoin comes up in sound-money thinking.

Limits and risks

Claim These are as important as the properties above:

  • Volatile: its price has risen and fallen sharply, at times within months. You can lose a large part of what you put in.
  • Capped supply is not a guarantee: it says nothing certain about future demand or purchasing power. Bitcoin itself produces no cash flow (any yield comes from third-party products with their own risks).
  • Custody risk: lost keys mean lost coins; mistakes and theft are usually irreversible; scams target newcomers; custodians can fail.
  • Practical limits: fees, limited transaction capacity, and the need for devices and connectivity.
  • Rules and taxes: regulation and tax treatment vary by country and can change.
  • Trade-offs: governance, security and energy use are actively debated.

Evidence The issuance schedule, the supply limit and the unit size are set by the Bitcoin software’s consensus rules: Satoshi Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System (2008), and the Bitcoin Core source code (a halving interval of 210,000 blocks; 100,000,000 base units per bitcoin; version 28.0). The paper describes the design; the exact numbers are set in the software. The limits and risks listed are general cautions, not research findings. A protocol's rules cannot show that Bitcoin protects purchasing power, beats other assets, or suits your household; those are claims about outcomes that need independent data. (The page What the evidence can say covers the BTC Helper model only, not Bitcoin in general.)

Decision Bitcoin may not be for you

All of these are valid outcomes: no; not now, because your household foundation comes first; or learn more before deciding. Nothing on this site needs you to own Bitcoin.

If you choose Bitcoin: questions to consider first

Value Our editorial safety boundary: household resilience comes before any optional purchase, and we don't treat borrowing to buy Bitcoin as responsible.

Decision Questions only you can answer:

  1. Is my household ready? What does the Household Planner show about your safety floor, your buffer and the money left each month? If there is no room, "not now" is a valid answer.
  2. Am I using borrowed money? See the boundary above.
  3. How large a fall could I live with?
  4. How would the coins be held, and what would it cost?
  5. Would a plan written in advance help me avoid decisions driven by price moves?

We don't suggest any amount or allocation. BTC Helper describes today's Bitcoin market conditions for people who already follow a written plan; it never establishes what your household can afford.
